The short version

An ETF can be easy to buy and hard to escape.
This dashboard measures that gap.

Start here

What is an ETF?

Imagine you want to invest in five hundred different American companies. Buying five hundred separate shareholdings would be slow, expensive, and impossible to manage.

So instead, a company assembles the basket for you. It buys all five hundred shareholdings, puts them in one container, and sells you a slice of that container. That container is an Exchange Traded Fund, or ETF. You buy and sell your slice on the stock exchange, in one click, the same way you would trade a single company's shares.

ETFs are genuinely one of the better financial inventions of the last few decades. They are cheap, transparent, and they let ordinary people own a diversified portfolio without needing a private banker. Nothing in this project argues otherwise.

But there is a structural quirk inside them that almost nobody looks at.

The problem

The basket and its contents move at different speeds.

Your slice of the fund trades on the stock exchange. It can change hands in a fraction of a second, thousands of times a day.

The things inside the basket trade in their own separate markets. Some of them, like Apple or Microsoft shares, trade enormously and can absorb almost any order instantly. Others barely trade at all: a corporate bond issued by a mid-sized company might go days without a single transaction. A small foreign-listed company might trade a few thousand shares in a session.

The wrapper is liquid. The contents may not be. On a calm day, nobody notices.

On a normal day this mismatch is invisible. Buyers and sellers of the fund roughly balance each other out, and the fund barely has to touch what it holds.

Why it matters

The problem only appears when everyone leaves at once.

When far more people sell the fund than buy it, the fund cannot simply shrug. Specialist firms called Authorised Participants hand fund units back to the issuer and receive the underlying securities, which they then sell. To meet sustained selling, the actual holdings have to be sold.

Now the mismatch becomes real. The fund is being sold in seconds. Its contents can only be sold over days or weeks without crashing their own prices.

Three things follow, in order:

  1. The fund's price detaches from what it actually owns. It starts trading below the true value of its holdings, because the market can see the exit is narrow.
  2. The fund is forced to sell whatever it can sell, which is usually its best and most liquid holdings. What remains is a worse portfolio for everyone still invested.
  3. In severe cases, redemptions are suspended entirely. Investors are locked in and cannot get their money out at all.

That third outcome is not hypothetical. UK investors in several high-profile property and equity funds have been frozen out for months, in some cases years, because the funds owned buildings and unlisted holdings that could not be sold at the speed investors were demanding their money back. During the March 2020 market panic, high-yield bond ETFs traded at material discounts to the value of their own holdings while the underlying bond market seized up.

A fire exit is only as wide as the crowd it has to clear.

The common mistake

Most liquidity checks measure the wrong thing.

Ask a standard screening tool whether an ETF is liquid and it will tell you how much the fund itself trades. Large daily volume, tight pricing, no problem.

That number tells you how easy the fund is to trade on a calm day. It tells you almost nothing about what happens on a bad one, because on a bad day the constraint is not the fund's own market. It is the market for everything inside it.

That is the illusion this project is named after.

What this does

This tool opens the basket and measures the contents.

By law, American funds must publish exactly what they hold, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission on a form called N-PORT. It is public, free, and almost nobody reads it.

This dashboard does four things:

  1. Downloads a fund's actual holdings from those regulatory filings.
  2. Pulls the real trading history for every single holding inside it.
  3. Measures how hard each one would be to sell, three different ways.
  4. Combines them, weighted by how much of the fund each holding represents, into a single score out of 100.

A low score means the fund could realistically unwind its positions without dislocating prices. A high score means it is holding things it cannot get out of quickly, and its calm surface is doing a lot of work.

The measurements

The three things we measure, in plain English.

Days to Liquidate

How long would it take to get out?

Every stock has a normal daily trading volume. A fund cannot dump its entire position into one day without becoming the whole market and crushing the price against itself. The industry rule of thumb is that it can safely account for about a fifth of a stock's normal daily volume.

So: if a fund owns 10 million shares of a company that normally trades 1 million shares a day, it can responsibly sell about 200,000 a day. Getting out takes 50 trading days. That is ten weeks, during which anything can happen.

This is the metric we weight most heavily, because it is the one that physically breaks first in a crisis.

Amihud illiquidity

How much does the price move when you trade?

In a deep, liquid market you can trade a large amount and the price barely reacts. In a thin one, a modest order sends the price lurching.

Amihud measures exactly that: how far the price moves for each dollar traded. A higher number means the holding is fragile, and that selling it in size would be self-defeating.

Estimated bid-ask spread

What does it cost you just to trade at all?

At any moment there are two prices for anything: what buyers are willing to pay, and what sellers are asking. The gap between them is the spread, and it is a real cost you pay the instant you trade, like the difference between the buy and sell rates at an airport currency desk.

Liquid holdings have a spread so thin it is almost invisible. Illiquid ones have a wide one, and that width is a direct measure of how reluctant the market is to take the other side of your trade.

Reading the result

What the score means.

The three measurements are combined for every holding, weighted by how much of the fund that holding represents, and scaled to a number out of 100.

The number on its own is less interesting than what sits behind it. The tool always shows you which specific holdings drive the score, because "this fund scores 47" is a fact, while "this fund scores 47 and two positions account for most of it" is something you can act on.

Honesty

What this cannot tell you.

Any tool that only ever flatters itself should be distrusted. Four limits worth stating up front:

The holdings data is a snapshot, not a live feed. Funds file their holdings quarterly, so you are looking at the most recent filing, not this morning's portfolio. The filing date is displayed alongside every score.

Some holdings cannot be measured at all. A fund may hold securities with no reliable public trading data, typically thinly traded foreign listings and most bonds. Those get excluded from the calculation. This matters enormously, because the holdings that are hardest to find data on are almost by definition the hardest to sell. Every score here is therefore a floor on the true risk, never a ceiling. The dashboard always shows you how many holdings it could and could not price.

The weighting is a judgement. The three measurements are blended in a deliberate proportion, and reasonable analysts would choose differently. Rather than hide that, the Analyze tab lets you move the weights yourself and watch the score respond.

This is not investment advice. It is a research and demonstration tool. A high score is not a sell signal, and a low one is not an endorsement. It measures one specific risk, under one specific set of assumptions, using free public data.

Sources

Where the numbers come from.

Everything here is built on free, public data. There is no paid terminal behind it.

  • SEC EDGAR (Form N-PORT) for what each fund actually holds.
  • Yahoo Finance for daily trading history of every holding.
  • NASDAQ Trader for the list of every ETF listed in the United States, which is how the market-wide scan gets its universe.

The estimated spread uses the Corwin-Schultz method, an academic technique for inferring trading costs from daily high and low prices when you do not have access to expensive tick-by-tick data.

Method

How every number on this site is made.

Two pipelines run here, and they answer different questions. The look-through opens a single fund and measures what it holds. The surface scan measures how the funds themselves trade, across the whole listed universe. Neither substitutes for the other, and the gap between them is the point of the project.

What would make this better

Three things money would buy.

  • Paid intraday TAQ data, so the spread is observed from the order book rather than inferred from daily bars.
  • Bond-level TRACE prints, which would let the bond funds — the ones where mismatch risk is highest — be measured at all.
  • Daily portfolio composition files from issuers, which would replace a quarterly filing with a current one.

Everything on this site is built on free public data, which is both the constraint and the argument: if a gap this large is visible without a terminal, it is not hidden. It is unexamined.
